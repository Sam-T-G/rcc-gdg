## In one sentence

Build the one sentence that makes a stranger with something to give you ask a follow-up question, and learn the three mechanics that separate the sentences that work from the ones that do not.

## Why this one

Arc 1 is about being understood by someone who has no reason to listen. This is the install: the mechanics, at the lowest possible stakes, with a partner the student chose. Week 2 adds a hostile listener, week 3 makes it about something they actually want, and week 4 sends it to a real person. Everything downstream is this sentence under load.

The hook session in week H2 compressed an existing answer. This week builds one on purpose, which is a different job.

## Materials

- Cards and pens.
- The three mechanics on one slide, up the whole hour.
- A timer.

## 2:30 Open (5 min)

- Club Rush results: Discord joins, table counts, one sentence on how it went.
- This is week 1 of a four-week arc. Say the arc out loud: Voice, then Ask in mid October, then Evidence through Demo Day on December 10.
- Week 4 of this arc (October 8) ends with everyone sending a real message to a real person from this room. Flag it now so nobody is ambushed.

## 2:35 Warm rep (7 min)

Pairs, sixty seconds each way: **something you changed your mind about this year.**

## 2:42 The Rep (18 min)

The three mechanics, on screen:

1. **Specific beats important.** "I am interested in healthcare technology" is important and says nothing. "I want to know why the intake form at the campus clinic takes eleven minutes" is specific and gets a conversation.
2. **A number beats an adjective.** "A lot of tutoring experience" against "I have tutored forty-one people through the same chemistry unit." The second one is answerable. Adjectives are unanswerable, which is why they end conversations.
3. **Name a person, not a category.** Who is worse off if you never do this? Say them.

Then, in trios, three passes of eight minutes total:

- **Pass 1 (3 min):** each person says a first draft. Partners say only which mechanic is missing. No advice.
- **Pass 2 (3 min):** rewrite using the missing mechanic. Say it again. Partners again name what is still missing.
- **Pass 3 (2 min):** final version, said standing.

Then five minutes to write it on a card, plus the follow-up question you hope it produces. Both go on the card. The second line is the test: if you cannot predict the question, the sentence is not doing its job.

## 3:00 Live fire (12 min)

Three volunteers. Each says their sentence. Before the room reacts, the facilitator asks the room: **what is the question you want to ask them?**

Compare the room's actual questions against the one the speaker wrote on their card. A match means the sentence works. A miss is the most useful data of the hour and is not a failure; say so.

Then two trues and a turn on each.

## 3:12 Debrief and rung (10 min)

1. Whose sentence produced a question they did not predict? What did the room hear that you did not say?
2. Which mechanic was hardest to add? Almost always the number, because it requires having counted something.
3. What would you have to go do this month so that a better number exists by December?

That last question is the seed of the Evidence arc in November. Let it sit; do not answer it.

**The rung, ten seconds:** the technical version is the one-line description at the top of a project. Same three mechanics, same failure mode. Most of them say "a web app for productivity" and die there.

## 3:22 The Ask (5 min)

Say the sentence to two people before next Thursday, and write down the question each of them asked back. Bring both to week 2, because week 2 uses this sentence against a listener who does not want to hear it.

## Failure modes

- **The room writes paragraphs.** One sentence. Enforce it out loud. A paragraph is avoidance.
- **Trios drift into advice.** The whole rep is naming what is missing, not fixing it for someone. Cut advice on sight.
- **Someone has no number.** Good. Their turn is "go count something this week." That is a real assignment and it works.
- **A student's honest answer is "I don't know what I want."** Legitimate and common. Have them build the sentence around the question they are actually carrying. "I do not know whether I want the clinical side or the technical side and I am trying to find someone who has done both" is a strong sentence.

## Record

Attendance count, how many cards written with both lines filled.
