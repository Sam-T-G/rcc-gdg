Before anything else, one quick question.

Your call

How would you fix parking at RCC?

Shout it out. First idea that comes to mind.

Take five or six answers fast and write them on the board. Keep that list up all hour; you come back to it at the end. Expect things like: build more parking, a parking garage, a parking app, carpool, take the bus. Don’t judge any of them.

Now look at a parking lot from above.

Look closer

From up here, you can see open spots.

From your car, you can’t. So is the problem too few spots, or not knowing where they are?

This is a lot in Denver, not RCC. Point at the empty spaces between the full rows. A driver on the ground sees full rows and keeps circling.

Say it once and move on: if there are too few spots, you build a garage. If people can’t find the open ones, you show them where they are. Different why, different fix. We don’t know RCC’s real numbers, and that’s the point: you have to find out.

First, you need a problem.

What annoyed you this week?

5 minutes · in your group

  1. Everyone names one thing.
  2. Pick one you’ve felt yourself.
  3. Stuck? Try parking or scalpers.
  4. Check: Who do you know who has it?

Press T to start the clock, then press next about once a minute; each press adds a line and the clock keeps going. Go first: say one real thing that annoyed you this week, so people see small counts.

More from the companion’s problem list if a group is stuck: textbook access codes, getting into a full class, splitting bills with roommates, finding a quiet place to study, the group project where one person does it all, food thrown out while students skip meals.

Challenges to pose while you walk: “Is that a problem, or did it only happen once?” “Who else has it?”

Now figure out why it happens.

Why does it happen?

8 minutes · one question at a time

  1. Who has it? Be exact.
  2. When is it worst?
  3. What do they do about it now?
  4. Why hasn’t anyone fixed it?
  5. Write it: ___ can’t ___ because ___.

Press T, then add a line about every 90 seconds. If a group is stuck, say the parking version out loud. Who: a student who drives in for a 9:00 class. When: right before 9, when every row looks full. Now: they circle, or park far away and run. Why: nobody can see where the open spots are. The sentence: “Students with 9:00 classes can’t find a spot because they can’t see which ones are open.”

The “because” is where the ideas come from. For tickets: “Fans can’t buy at face value because bots buy faster than any person can click.” Ticketmaster’s Verified Fan presale is a real fix built on that because: fans sign up ahead of time, bots and known resellers are screened out, and picked fans get a one-time code (Billboard; Music Week).

Challenges to pose: “How do you know? Did someone tell you, or are you guessing?” “Is that the real why, or the first one? Ask why again.”

Now, ideas. A lot of them.

Crazy 8s

Eight ideas in eight minutes

Fold a sheet into eight boxes. One idea per box.

Crazy 8s is a standard design sprint exercise (Open Practice Library, openpracticelibrary.com/practice/crazy-8s; Google’s Design Sprint Kit has a page on it too). Fold a sheet in half three times and you get eight boxes. Everyone does their own sheet.

Point at the tags: only three of these eight parking ideas are apps. Words or doodles both count.

Paper out. I’ll add a push every minute or two.

Fill all eight boxes.

8 minutes · on your own

  1. One idea per box. No talking yet.
  2. Box 3: How would an airline do it?
  3. Box 4: Who’d fix this for money?
  4. Box 5: Fix it with a new rule.
  5. Box 7: Your weirdest idea.

Press T. Add each push when the clock shows about 6:00, 5:00, 4:00, and 2:00, so the box number matches the minute. Stalling at four is normal; the last four are usually the interesting ones.

Pushes to say out loud while you walk: “What if they never had to do the annoying part?” “Make one that’s free.” “Who would pay to make this go away?”

Pens down. Now pick one as a group.

Pick one and build it out.

6 minutes · in your group

  1. Share your best two, then vote.
  2. Give it a name.
  3. How does it work? Three steps.
  4. Who pays for it?
  5. Check: Look it up. Does it exist?

Everyone gets two votes; mixing two ideas is fine. Three steps means from the user’s side: what they tap, see, or do, not how the tech works. Who pays can be the users, a business, the school, or ads.

On the last line: most ideas already exist in some form, and that’s normal. Ask what theirs does that the other one doesn’t, or who the other one misses.

Now start walking with the challenge cards. Ask each group what kind of idea it is (an app, something that connects people, a rule, a physical thing, a service) and read them one card for that kind.

Now let’s see what happens to these ideas in real life.

What would actually happen?

Challenge checks · 5 minutes

  1. Who are your first 10 users?
  2. It costs money every month. Who pays?
  3. The school says no. What’s plan B?
  4. A big company copies you. Now what?
  5. Would people trust it with their info?

Press T, then add a check every minute. Groups don’t answer all of them. Each group picks the one that hurts their idea most, changes the idea to handle it, and writes down what they changed on page 2. That change goes in the pitch.

Keep walking with the challenge cards. The best challenge is one made for their idea: “Your app needs drivers and riders. Who signs up first?” “Your sign needs power. Where does it come from?”

Last step before the pitches.

Plan your pitch with STAR

60 seconds, in this order

  1. S · Who has the problem, and when?
  2. T · What has to change? Your because.
  3. A · What you built, in three steps.
  4. R · What gets better, and who pays.
  5. Then one challenge you fixed.

Same STAR as last week, used for a pitch instead of a story. Situation is the problem scene, Task is what has to change, Action is the product, Result is what gets better. Same rule as last week too: spend most of the time on the A.

Five minutes. Pick one person to pitch, or split it by letter. The companion’s step 6 fills this outline in from everything the group typed, and its practice view runs a 60-second clock; the worksheet’s page 2 has it too.

Okay. First group, you’re up.

Pitches · 60 seconds each

Pitch it with STAR.

1:00

Situation, task, action, result, then one challenge you fixed. After that, one question from the room.

For each group: R resets the clock, T starts it. Write each product’s name on the board as they go; the vote reads from that list. One question from the room per group. If nobody asks, pick a challenge card they didn’t answer.

About 10 minutes for pitches (3:16 to 3:26), which fits six or seven groups at 60 seconds plus one question each. With more groups, cut each pitch to 45 seconds.

Every group’s done. Time to vote.

Vote

Which one would you actually use?

Hands up for each one. You can vote for more than one.

Read each name off the board and count hands. Circle the top two.

Look at the top two. Where did those ideas come from?

What just happened

The best ideas came from the why.

Compare them to the parking ideas we shouted at the start.

Ask the top two groups to read their “because” out loud. Usually the winning idea answers it directly. Then point at the parking list from the start: most of those were guesses made before anyone asked why.
