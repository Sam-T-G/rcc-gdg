Before anything else, one quick question.

Your call

How would you fix parking at RCC?

Shout it out. First idea that comes to mind.

Take five or six answers fast and write them on the board. Keep that list up all hour; you come back to it at the end. Expect things like: build more parking, a parking garage, a parking app, carpool, take the bus. Don’t judge any of them.

Now look at a parking lot from above.

Look closer

From up here, you can see open spots.

From your car, you can’t. So is the problem too few spots, or not knowing where they are?

This is a lot in Denver, not RCC. Point at the empty spaces between the full rows. A driver on the ground sees full rows and keeps circling.

We don’t know RCC’s real numbers, and that’s the point: you can’t tell which problem it is until you find out.

And those two problems need very different fixes.

The fix depends on the why

If there aren’t enough spots

Build more spots, like a new garage.

If people can’t find the open ones

Show them the open spots before they leave home.

Point at the board: most of the room’s ideas probably fix the left side. That isn’t wrong. It’s just the first guess. Click for the right side.

Groups of four. One person writes for the group. No code and no laptops; phones away except for looking something up. At about 3:08, every group pitches.

First, you need a problem.

In your group · 5 minutes

What annoyed you this week?

5:00

Everyone names one thing, big or small. Write them all down. In the last minute, pick the one your group wants to fix.

Go first: say one real thing that annoyed you this week, so people see small counts. Start the clock with T. Walk around. If a group is stuck, ask: what did you wait in line for this week? What cost more than it should? What did you do twice?

Here’s how to dig into a problem, using parking.

Four questions, using parking

Ask

The question

For parking

  1. WhoWho has it? Be exact.A student who drives in for a 9:00 class.
  2. WhenWhen is it worst?Right before 9, when every row looks full.
  3. NowWhat do they do now?Circle the lot, or park far away and run.
  4. WhyWhy isn’t it fixed?Nobody can see where the open spots are.

A made-up example, not RCC data. Click once per row. The last row matters most. If a group can’t answer “why,” they haven’t found the problem yet. “Now” matters too: whatever people do today is what your product has to beat.

Then you put all four into one sentence.

Then say it in one sentence

What people say

After digging

Parking

Parking here is the worst.

Students with 9:00 classes can’t find a spot because they can’t see which ones are open.

Tickets

Scalpers ruin concerts.

Fans can’t buy at face value because bots buy faster than any person can click.

Who can’t do what Because

Two presses. The pattern is: someone can’t do something, because of a reason. The “because” is where the ideas come from.

For tickets, the because points at speed, so a fix is to make speed not matter. A real one: Ticketmaster’s Verified Fan presale has fans sign up ahead of time, screens out bots and known resellers, and sends the fans it picks a one-time code (Billboard; Music Week). Mention it only if a group picks tickets.

Your turn. Ten minutes.

Dig into your problem

Ten minutes

  1. 1. Who has it? Be exact.
  2. 2. When is it worst?
  3. 3. What do they do about it now?
  4. 4. Why hasn’t anyone fixed it?
  5. 5. Write it: ___ can’t ___ because ___.

Officers walk around and ask: “Who exactly? A freshman? A parent?” and “How do you know?” If a group’s why is “nobody cares,” ask who would have to care and why they don’t. Groups that finish early: ask what someone with the problem would say if they heard the sentence.

Now, ideas. A lot of them.

Crazy 8s

Eight ideas in eight minutes

Fold a sheet into eight boxes. One idea per box, about a minute each.

Crazy 8s is a standard design sprint exercise (Open Practice Library, openpracticelibrary.com/practice/crazy-8s; Google’s Design Sprint Kit has a page on it too). Fold a sheet in half three times and you get eight boxes.

These eight are parking ideas, and only three of them are apps. Read a few out loud: a live map of open spots, signs at each gate that show how many spots are left, valet parking, carpool matches by class time, staggered class start times, a shuttle from the far lot, reserving a spot the night before, and premium spots near the buildings that cost more. Words or quick doodles both count; nobody is grading drawings.

Around idea four, most people get stuck. Try these.

When you get stuck

Try one of these

  1. Borrow it.How would an airline, a restaurant, or a dating app handle this?
  2. Pay someone.Who would fix this for money? Now it’s a business.
  3. Change a rule.Could a new rule fix it with no app at all?
  4. Skip the step.What if they never had to do the annoying part?

Each one matches a box on the example sheet. Premium spots are borrowed from airline seats. Valet parking is paying someone. Staggered class times are a rule. Reserving a spot skips the circling.

Officers: during the clock, walk around and ask these out loud to anyone who’s stopped writing.

Paper out. Eight minutes, on your own.

On your own · 8 minutes

Fill all eight boxes.

8:00

One idea per box. Weird ideas count. At least one can’t be an app. No talking until time’s up.

Everyone does their own sheet, including the group’s writer. Call out each minute (“Box three!”) so people keep moving. Stalling at four is normal; the last four are usually the interesting ones.

Pens down. Now pick one as a group.

In your group · 3 minutes

Read your best two out loud. Then vote.

Everyone gets two votes. The most votes wins. Mixing two ideas is fine.

About two minutes to read and one to vote. People vote by marking the sheets. If one idea isn’t a clear winner, let the group combine the top two.

Now turn the winner into a product.

Make it a product

Your product card

  1. 1. Name it.
  2. 2. The problem, in one sentence.
  3. 3. How it works, in three steps.
  4. 4. Why it beats what they do now.
  5. 5. Who pays for it?

These five lines are page two of the printed worksheet. Line 2 is the sentence they already wrote, and it already says who the product is for. Line 3 is from the user’s side: what they tap, see, or do, not how the tech works. For line 5, the payer can be the users, a business, the school, or ads. “Nobody” is a real answer too, but then say who keeps it running.

Pick one person to pitch. Your card is the script.

Pitches · 60 seconds each

Read your card, top to bottom.

1:00

Name, problem, how it works, why it’s better, who pays. Then one question from the room.

For each group: R resets the clock, T starts it. Write each product’s name on the board as they go; the vote reads from that list. One question from the room per group. If nobody asks, ask “What could kill this?”

About 14 minutes for pitches. With more than seven groups, cut each pitch to 45 seconds.

Every group’s done. Time to vote.

Vote

Which one would you actually use?

Hands up for each one. You can vote for more than one.

Read each name off the board and count hands. Circle the top two.

Look at the top two. Where did those ideas come from?

What just happened

The best ideas came from the why.

Compare them to the parking ideas we shouted at the start.

Ask the top two groups to read their “because” out loud. Usually the winning idea answers the because directly. Then point at the parking list from the start: most of those were guesses made before anyone asked why.

This is the first step of every hackathon and every startup pitch: start from the problem, not the app.
