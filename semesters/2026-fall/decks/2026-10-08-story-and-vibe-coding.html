Your list already has a name.

Your list has a name

STAR. Situation, Task, Action, Result.

It started as a job interview method. Interviewers still use it.

Match their answers to the letters: “what happened” is the Action, “how it ended” is the Result. STAR came out of Development Dimensions International (DDI) in the 1970s as a behavioral interview method (ddi.com, behavioral interviewing). MIT’s career office teaches it for interview answers (capd.mit.edu, “The STAR method for behavioral interviews”). “Tell me about a time when…” is the question it answers.

Here’s what each letter means, and how long each part should be.

Each letter

Spend most of your time on the A.

  1. Situation.Where and when. Keep it short.
  2. Task.Your job. The problem or the goal.
  3. Action.What you did. Say “I,” not “we.”
  4. Result.What changed. A number, a reaction, what came next.

Bar lengths are approximate: short setup, long action, clear result. The most common mistake is spending half the story on the Situation.

Four common mistakes.

Avoid these

Common STAR mistakes

  1. Too much setup.Half the time on the S. Say what you did sooner.
  2. Only “we.”The team did great. What did you do?
  3. No ending.If nothing changed, say what you learned.
  4. Naming the quality.“I’m very responsible.” Show it with the story.

Go fast. “We” is fine for context; your Action needs “I.” Same as last week: show the quality, don’t name it.

While that runs: what is vibe coding?

Where the name comes from

The name is from February 2025.

Andrej Karpathy came up with it. Collins Dictionary made it the 2025 Word of the Year.

Karpathy, an OpenAI co-founder, coined “vibe coding” in a post in February 2025. Collins Dictionary named it Word of the Year 2025 (announced November 6, 2025; CNN, Collins Language Blog). The quote is Collins’s: “It’s programming by vibes, not variables.” Collins definition: using AI prompted by natural language to help write code.

Once it’s running, here’s how to ask for changes.

Best practices

How to ask for changes

  1. One change per message.Five asks at once and you can’t tell what broke.
  2. Say what you see.“The button covers the title on my phone,” not “fix it.”
  3. Click on it.Annotation mode: click part of the preview, then say the change.
  4. Ask it to explain.“What did you just change, and why?” You learn how it works.

Annotation mode is in Google’s Build mode docs: highlight a UI element and describe what you want. Show it in the demo if you can find it.

And when it breaks, which it will…

When it breaks

It will break. That’s normal.

  1. Paste the error.Copy the exact message into the chat.
  2. Go back to a working version.Use Restore. Don’t keep adding fixes.
  3. Start over if it’s a mess.New chat, clearer first prompt. Often faster.
  4. No private info.No passwords, keys, or personal info.

Restore: each turn in the Build chat can be restored to that version. Known quirks from Google’s developer forum: restore points can vanish after a page refresh, and the chat still remembers what you undid, so after a restore, tell it plainly what to forget, or start a new chat.
