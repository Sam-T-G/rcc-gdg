There’s a name for your list

STAR. Situation, Task, Action, Result.

It started as a way to run job interviews. Interviewers still listen for it.

Map their shouted answers onto the letters: “what happened” is the Action, “how it ended” is the Result. STAR came out of Development Dimensions International (DDI) in the 1970s as a behavioral interview method (ddi.com, behavioral interviewing). MIT’s career office teaches it for interview answers (capd.mit.edu, “The STAR method for behavioral interviews”). “Tell me about a time when…” is the question it answers.

Here’s what each letter asks for, and how long it gets.

One letter at a time

Most of it is the A.

  1. Situation.Where and when. Just enough to follow.
  2. Task.What was on you. The problem or the goal.
  3. Action.What you did. Say “I,” not “we.”
  4. Result.What changed. A number, a reaction, what came next.

Bar lengths are a rule of thumb, not a formula: setup short, action long, result clear. The most common miss is spending half the story on the Situation.

Four ways these stories usually go wrong.

Watch for these

Where STAR stories go wrong

  1. All setup.Half the time on the S. Get to what you did.
  2. We, we, we.The team did great. What did you do?
  3. No ending.If nothing changed, say what you learned.
  4. Saying the quality.“I’m very responsible.” Let the story say it.

Quick. “We” is fine for context; your Action needs “I.” Last week’s rule carries over: if you have to say it, you haven’t shown it.

Here’s the trick most people miss.

The trick

STAR is how you tell it. A resume line flips it.

Lead with what you did and what changed. The setup shrinks to a few words.

Out loud, STAR goes in time order. On paper, a recruiter skims (7 to 15 seconds on the first pass, from last week), so the result and the verb go first. Sam’s resume guide: STAR is for interviews, not bullets; written out in full it bloats the setup past one line.

How it actually goes

Small loops, not one giant prompt.

Describe, run it, try it like a user, then steer with one change. Repeat until you like it.

Once it’s running, here’s how to steer.

Best practices

How to steer it

  1. One change per message.Five asks at once and you can’t tell what broke.
  2. Say what you see.“The button covers the title on my phone,” not “fix it.”
  3. Point at it.Annotation mode: click part of the preview, then say the change.
  4. Ask it to explain.“What did you just change, and why?” You learn as you go.

Annotation mode is in Google’s Build mode docs: highlight a UI element and describe what you want. Show it live in the demo if it’s where you expect it.

And when it breaks, because it will…

When it breaks

It will break. That’s normal.

  1. Paste the error.Copy the exact message into the chat.
  2. Go back, don’t pile on.Restore the last version that worked instead of stacking fixes.
  3. Start fresh if it’s tangled.New chat, clearer first prompt. Often faster.
  4. Keep secrets out.No passwords, keys, or personal info. Ever.

Restore: each turn in the Build chat can be restored to that version. Known quirks from Google’s developer forum: restore points can vanish after a page refresh, and the chat still remembers what you undid, so after a restore, tell it plainly what to forget, or start a new chat.
