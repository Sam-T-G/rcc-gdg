Once it’s filled in, there’s a trick most people don’t know about.

Then print it

Your profile, saved as a PDF, is resume draft one.

  1. Me, then View profile.
  2. Hit the ⋯ button, then Save to PDF.On a laptop. The app can’t do it.
  3. Sam’s came out 7 pages.Cut it to one. That’s your resume, version one.

Done for real on Sam’s profile on 2026-09-30: Me > View profile > the ⋯ button (labeled More, next to Add custom button) > Save to PDF. The old Help article (a541960) says “More or Resources”; the button is ⋯ now. The download was a 7-page PDF, which is the point: it prints everything, and the resume is the one-page cut. Desktop only, English profiles only, 200 PDFs a month (Help a541960).

Before you hit Connect on everyone, a few rules.

How it works

How to connect without being weird

  1. Add a note.One line: where you met, or why you’re reaching out. Free accounts get 3 notes a month.
  2. Don’t pitch first.Ask a question. Nobody wants a sales pitch from a stranger.
  3. Reply and thank.A thank-you after a chat or an intro is what people remember.
  4. Keep it professional.It’s the one feed your future boss also reads.

Note limit from LinkedIn Help (a563153): up to three personalized invitations a month on a free account, 200 characters each. Blogs still say five; the live page says three.

One rule to check every line against.

One rule

If you have to say it, you haven’t shown it.

Cut: hard-working, passionate, detail-oriented, team player, responsible for, helped with.

From Sam’s bullet-pattern notes: every adjective you use about yourself gets cut and replaced with a behavior and a result.

No entries yet?

Reverse-engineer your resume.

Start from what you want it to say. Then go earn the line.

For anyone thinking “I have nothing to put on this.” Nobody starts with entries. You pick what you want the page to prove, then go get the entry that proves it.

Here’s how to work backward.

How it works

Work backward from the line

  1. Pick your signal.What trait do you want this page to prove?
  2. Find the lead.A club, a class project, the tutoring lab, a hackathon, a professor.
  3. Decide the result.What do you want to be able to say happened?
  4. Earn the line.Do it, then write it as what changed.

Signal first: decide what you want to prove, then pick the door that proves it, then decide what outcome you’re going for, so you know what to count while you do it. The next slide shows Sam’s entries in that same order.
